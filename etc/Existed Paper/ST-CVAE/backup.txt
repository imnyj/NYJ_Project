\begin{abstract}
The proliferation of autonomous driving and immersive multimedia services in vehicular networks has led to an exponential increase in data traffic, challenging the capacity of limited backhaul links.
While precaching at Roadside Units (RSUs) has emerged as a promising solution to mitigate latency, existing mobility-aware schemes often suffer from deterministic prediction errors and inefficient data collection, leading to either service interruptions or excessive wasted traffic.
In this paper, we propose a novel precaching scheme based on a Spatio-Temporal Conditional Variational Autoencoder (ST-CVAE) that captures the stochastic and multi-modal nature of vehicular mobility.
Unlike traditional approaches requiring continuous trajectory tracking, our scheme utilizes instantaneous communication snapshots, reducing wireless congestion.
The ST-CVAE, enhanced with cyclical temporal encoding, generates probabilistic dwell time estimates, allowing RSUs to determine conservative precaching thresholds that handle uncertainty.
Furthermore, we implement a decentralized learning framework with an asynchronous model update mechanism, ensuring that RSUs can adapt to local traffic conditions without central aggregation or service downtime.
Simulation results demonstrate that our proposed scheme outperforms baseline methods in terms of prediction accuracy, cache hit ratio, and backhaul efficiency.
The source code of ST-CVAE is available at https://github.com/ksnu-kimjh/ST-CVAE.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} rapid advent of autonomous and connected vehicles has fundamentally shifted in-car behavior, drawing occupants' attention away from driving tasks and towards immersive multimedia applications, which are derived from high-definition sensor feeds and real-time map updates, as well as video streaming and interactive gaming \cite{Zhe2020, yuan2018}.
At the same time, advances in display technologies and richer content formats have driven up both the bitrate and size of each content \cite{Kumer2013, Pranav2019, Reichardt2002, Yen2020}.
According to the Ericsson Report, global mobile data traffic was 130 EB per month at the end of 2023, and is projected to reach 280 EB per month by the end of 2030, representing an estimated 2.15-fold increase \cite{Ericsson}.
Since this surge places unprecedented strain on conventional cellular infrastructures, playback stalls and buffer under-runs introduce perceptible delays when vehicles generate successive requests that exceed a base station’s processing capacity.
In situations where safety is of paramount importance, such as collision-avoidance alerts or cooperative intersection crossing, even minor delays can result in meters of uncontrolled travel, which poses a significant risk to passengers and pedestrians.
Moreover, inconsistent response times erode trust in advanced driver‑assist systems and slow the transition to fully autonomous fleets.
These challenges are most acute in dense urban environments, where vehicles frequently switch between small cells or roadside units (RSUs), breaking active sessions and compounding end‑to‑end delay.
Without proactive measures to push content closer to the vehicle, next‑generation vehicular networks risk becoming bottlenecked by their own traffic, undermining both safety and user experience.

\IEEEpubidadjcol
In order to address these limitations, researchers have investigated the transition from conventional IP-based models to content-centric paradigms that decouple data from specific servers and deliver it by name.
The Content-Centric Networking (CCN) paradigm \cite{Jacobson2009, zhang2014} is predicated on the approach that routers along the delivery path cache named data objects, so that subsequent requests for the same content can be served from the nearest cache rather than the origin server.
The efficiency of in-network caching in reducing redundant transmissions, balancing load across the network, and decreasing end-to-end latency by eliminating unnecessary hops has been demonstrated.
An extension of the CCN to vehicular environments, termed the content-centric Internet of Vehicles (CIoV), leverages RSUs and onboard caches as cooperative nodes, dynamically storing popular data according to vehicle mobility patterns \cite{Li2017, Hichri2021}.
In CIoV, precaching strategies proactively distribute content chunks to RSUs based on predicted demand to smooth handovers and minimize session breaks during RSU transitions.
However, naive or static precaching policies, which rely on simple heuristics or fixed popularity thresholds, are unable to keep pace with rapidly changing vehicular trajectories, unpredictable inter-arrival times, and shifting user preferences, giving rise to cache misses in high-demand zones or the accumulation of underutilized data.

To overcome these limitations, recent schemes have integrated machine learning (ML) into the precaching pipeline, empowering RSUs to make data-based caching decisions \cite{Ding2022, Wu2022, AlNagar2022}.
Supervised learning algorithms leverage features such as vehicular speed profiles, RSU queue lengths, traffic signal phases, and historical content request patterns to forecast both per-vehicle dwell times and content requests.
These data-based forecasts enable RSUs to dynamically provide an optimal number of content chunks for each vehicle.
The efficiency of ML-driven precaching is, however, highly relied on prediction accuracy.
Overestimating dwell time leads to wasted traffic that consumes valuable bandwidth and cache space, whereas underestimating results in playback stalls and aggravated end-to-end delay.
To mitigate these risks, uncertainty quantification techniques, such as Monte Carlo Dropout, deep ensemble learning, and quantile regression, are employed to bound prediction errors and decide conservative caching when model confidence is low.
Despite these advances, ML-driven precaching still faces practical hurdles.
High-fidelity models demand large, diverse training datasets, and complex techniques impose substantial computational and memory overhead on RSUs that have constrained resources.
Additionally, the treatment of dwell time predictions as independent tasks with static uncertainty configurations may not adapt to rapidly changing vehicular dynamics.
Furthermore, the cumulative delays inherent in the processes of feature extraction, inference, and uncertainty calibration have the potential to nullify the latency benefits that are the primary objective of precaching.

In this paper, we propose a novel precaching scheme based on a Spatio-Temporal Conditional Variational Autoencoder (ST-CVAE), designed to explicitly capture the stochastic and multi-modal nature of vehicular mobility in urban environments.
Unlike conventional deterministic models that average distinct traffic modes, such as passing through a green light versus stopping at a red signal, our generative framework learns the full conditional probability distribution of dwell times.
To enhance feature representation, we incorporate a Cyclical Encoding (CYE) module that preserves the temporal continuity of traffic signal phases and a ReGLU-augmented Residual Backbone optimized for heterogeneous tabular data.
Based on the probabilistic dwell time estimates, RSUs dynamically determine the optimal amount of content to serve locally and to precache at the next hop, incorporating a safety buffer to mitigate prediction uncertainty.
Furthermore, we implement a decentralized, event-driven feedback mechanism where vehicles report their actual mobility outcomes, allowing RSUs to asynchronously update their local models without disrupting ongoing services.
This approach minimizes both service interruptions and redundant backhaul traffic by making conservative yet precise caching decisions.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section \ref{sec2} reviews the related work on content delivery and precaching schemes in CIoV.
In Section \ref{sec.net}, we describe the vehicular network environment and provide an overview of the proposed ST-CVAE-based precaching scheme.
Then, Section \ref{sec.prop} details the core operations of the proposed scheme, including decentralized feature acquisition, probabilistic dwell time estimation, and the asynchronous model update mechanism.
To validate the performance of the proposed scheme, Section \ref{sec.s} conducts extensive simulations reflecting realistic urban traffic conditions to evaluate both prediction accuracy and network-level efficiency.
Finally, Section \ref{sec.c} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work} \label{sec2}
In a general IoV system, content delivery remains tightly bound to host-centric IP routing, where communication is anchored to specific server address.
This design becomes problematic in high-mobility environments.
When several vehicles within the same RSU coverage simultaneously request identical content, the IP-based routing requires the server to establish and maintain a separate connection for each request.
Consequently, the same data pass through the backhaul multiple times, consuming more unnecessary resources.
The issue is further complicated by the frequent handover due to vehicle movement.
Each time a vehicle enters the coverage area of a new RSU, it must re-establish its connection with the content server, resulting in noticeable delays.
Furthermore, retrieving content from the content servers via long-distance lines exacerbates latency and increases the load on the backbone network, ultimately limiting the scalability of IP-based vehicle services.

\subsection{CIoV}
To address the limitation of IP-based vehicular networks, researchers have explored CCN as a promising alternative \cite{Amadeo12n, Su2017, Wang2019}.
CCN shifts the focus from the host to the data itself, utilizes named content, and enables caching within the network.
This design characteristic is highly suited to the dynamic vehicle scenario, characterized by short-term connectivity and frequent handovers.
Through pioneering research, \cite{Amadeo12s, Su2017} introduced a CCN-based framework for vehicular ad-hoc networks based on 802.11p WAVE.
This demonstrated that named content retrieval in highly mobile environments exhibits significantly superior performance compared to existing IP-based approaches.
Subsequent evaluation studies confirmed the effectiveness of the CCN architecture under diverse traffic and popularity conditions \cite{Amadeo13}.
\cite{Su2017} proposed Content-Centric Vehicular Networks(CCVNs) that add content-centric units to the RSU.
These units dynamically store and serve data based on popularity and vehicle density, updating pending interests through transmission pattern and network topology analysis.
Meanwhile, \cite{Wang2019} proposed a hybrid solution that maintains an IP-based unicast transmission method while introducing a content-centric addressing scheme based on geographical proximity.
This framework enables vehicles to request content based on location, allowing them to receive content from the nearest provider without relying on the reverse path.
This effectively reduces content acquisition costs and improves success rates.
These studies collectively laid the groundwork for the Content-centric Internet of Vehicles (CIoV).
Based on the CIoV, RSUs and vehicles act as cooperative caching nodes, alleviating the burden on centralized servers by delivering content by name.
However, CIoV faces practical constraints.
The integration of CCN into vehicular systems addressed several of the inefficiencies of host-IP models, yet it did not eliminate all performance barriers.
Even within a CIoV environment, where content is named, cached, and served from intermediate nodes, service quality degrades when requested content is unavailable locally.
This becomes particularly problematic during frequent handovers between RSUs, as each RSU maintains only a limited cache.
When a vehicle passes through an RSU rapidly, the likelihood that the next RSU already stores the required content is very low.
Consequently, download sessions may still be interrupted or require costly backhaul searches.

\subsection{Precaching in CIoV}
Therefore, the concept of precaching was proposed to enable RSUs, which are expected to serve the vehicle, to prepare content in advance \cite{Ostrovskaya2018, Amadeo2021, Dua2019, Park2019, Ahani2018, AlNagar2019, Liang2018, Grewe2018}.
Rather than responding to an Interest packet after the vehicle arrives, this ensures uninterrupted delivery by pre-supplying the necessary chunks to the next RSU.
The necessity of precaching becomes more pronounced in scenarios with high vehicle density and repetitive requests for similar multimedia content, such as video streaming or high-resolution maps.
When multiple vehicles request similar data while traversing the urban grid, pushing popular content closer to edge nodes reduces redundant backhaul transmission.
Thus, precaching simultaneously delivers latency benefits for individual vehicles and enhances bandwidth efficiency across the entire network.
Moreover, vehicle mobility that is constrained by road topology and signals is inherently predictable.
Consequently, leveraging this predictability by staging data along anticipated routes has emerged as a natural solution in CIoV research.
Various studies have begun systematizing this strategy, exploring popularity-based polices, cooperative caching designs, and mobility-aware staging techniques.

\subsubsection{Popularity-driven and cache-policy designs}
In the vehicle-based CCN environment, initial precaching strategies focused on determining which content should be precached on RSUs with finite storage capacity.
The most direct approach was popular-based caching.
Content with high inter-vehicle request frequency was proactively replicated to the RSU, as it was statistically more likely to be requested in the near future.
This fundamental principle was subsequently refined by several alternative and diversity-oriented methods.
\cite{Ostrovskaya2018} proposed the multi-metric content replacement policy (M$^{2}$CRP), which evaluates three dimensions: freshness, popularity, and spatial relevance.
The three values are measured by the distance between the content's reception location and storage location relative to an RSU's coverage.
By combining these criteria, M$^{2}$CRP aims to retain content that is not only popular but also temporally and spatially relevant, whilst discarding outdated or less pertinent items for vehicles in the area.
This reflects that vehicle requests are non-uniform, and an RSU's cache space must be adjusted to match demand characteristics at both temporal and spatial scales.
\cite{Amadeo2021} later advanced this line with the Diversity-improved cAchiNg of popular Transient contEnts (DANTE), which targeted the distribution of distinct but still popular items.
DANTE empowered vehicles and RSUs to autonomously make local caching decisions, introducing minor architectural modifications to packet fields so that nodes could communicate the residual lifetime and popularity of content.
This enabled caching nodes to avoid redundancy and instead maximize the diversity of transient items available in their vicinity.
Evaluations showed that distributing distinct yet popular transient contents reduced retrieval latency and backhaul strain more effectively than na\"ive replication of the same item at every RSU.
Another approach leveraged probabilistic data structures to improve efficiency.
\cite{Dua2019} designed a Bloom filter-based caching scheme to reduce the time complexity of insertion, deletion, and lookup operations in vehicular caches.
Bloom filters allowed RSUs and vehicles to quickly decide whether an item was likely to exist locally without resorting to expensive searches.
This was particularly valuable when vehicles acted as opportunistic caches themselves, cooperating with RSUs to distribute contents.
Finally, policies at the RSU level considered resource constraints such as limited memory and buffer time.
\cite{Park2019} introduced a mobility-aware distributed proactive caching scheme in which RSUs distributed popular contents among themselves, coordinated through local communication.
Instead of each RSU caching everything, caches were diversified across neighboring RSUs to reduce redundancy and collectively increase the coverage of popular items.
These popularity-based and policy-based approaches, when applied together, demonstrated that significant gains could be achieved by carefully selecting what to precache.
However, all approaches relied on heuristics and thresholds concerning content popularity, recency, or diversity, and failed to adapt dynamically to the rapidly changing mobility of vehicles.


\subsubsection{Mobility-aware proactive placement}
In parallel with popularity-driven schemes, another branch of research focused on where content should be staged and how many chunks of content should be precached, given that vehicles inevitably move from one RSU to another.
The core idea of mobility-aware precaching is to predict the next RSU a vehicle will connect to and then ensure that the RSU precaches the required content.
\cite{Ahani2018} examined the problem of optimal proactive caching with user mobility, explicitly formulating it as a resource allocation problem subject to constraints on buffer size, link capacity, and retention time.
Their model quantified how many chunks should be placed at each candidate RSU to minimize expected delay, balancing resource cost with service continuity.
\cite{AlNagar2019} further developed mobility-aware proactive caching strategies for VANETs, in which RSUs coordinate to decide which contents should be precached based on projected trajectories.
Their design sought to minimize latency while preventing unnecessary replication across all RSUs.
By exploiting trajectory knowledge, only the most likely next-hop RSUs cached the data, reducing backhaul waste.
\cite{Liang2018} proposed an adaptive data prefetching framework for 5G mobile networks with edge caching, generalizable to vehicular contexts.
Their system leveraged trajectory prediction to prefetch data at edge nodes likely to serve the user next.
In vehicular networks, this translates to proactively caching video or map data at RSUs along the predicted route, improving hit ratios while maintaining low average delay.
\cite{Grewe2018} designed the Adaptive Distributed Content Prefetching mechanism for Information-Centric Connected Vehicles (ADePt).
ADePt distributes content along potential future paths rather than only at the immediate next RSU, addressing uncertainties in exact vehicle movement.
This adaptive multi-hop prefetching increased the probability that whichever RSU the vehicle arrived at next would already hold the needed chunks.
\cite{Ikram2021} presented the Left-Right-Front (LRF) caching strategy, which proactively caches data at three RSUs, corresponding to the vehicle's potential next moves: a left turn, a right turn, or a forward move.
This strategy acknowledged the uncertainty of intersection behavior, and this hedged against misprediction by caching along multiple paths.
Results showed improvements in cache hit ratios and hop efficiency within ICN-based vehicular IoT.
These works demonstrated that leveraging mobility knowledge can sharply reduce session disruption.
However, they largely relied on deterministic or heuristic mobility prediction models, such as assuming vehicles always take the shortest path or using simple Markov probabilities of turns.
Such assumptions often failed under real-world irregularities like congestion, traffic signal delays, or rerouting, leading to unnecessary precaching at RSUs that the vehicle never visits.

Despite the advancements made by both popularity-driven and mobility-aware precaching strategies, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved.
First, most existing approaches are heavily heuristic, relying on predefined thresholds or static parameters such as popularity scores and freshness timers.
These parameters are not easily tuned in practice, and their effectiveness quickly diminishes under the fast-changing conditions of vehicular networks.
Second, the prediction of vehicle mobility is often coarse and unreliable.
In real traffic scenarios, vehicles may deviate from expected routes due to congestion, traffic signals, or sudden detours, which causes content to be cached at RSUs that the vehicle never reaches while leaving upcoming RSUs unprepared.
Third, finite storage available at RSUs further compounds the problem, since limited capacity makes it difficult to balance between keeping popular items and preparing data for imminent vehicle arrivals.
Finally, many schemes require frequent recalculation of caching decisions to remain effective, introducing additional computational and signaling overhead that reduces system responsiveness.

\subsection{ML-based Precaching}
The limitations of heuristic popularity-driven or trajectory-based precaching have led researchers to adopt ML as a foundation for intelligent cache management in CIoV.
Unlike static schemes, ML-based methods can continuously learn from mobility traces, traffic states, and content request patterns, enabling more accurate and adaptive predictions.
By framing caching as either a supervised learning or a reinforcement learning decision process, recent works have demonstrated that ML can substantially improve cache hit ratios, reduce delivery latency, and lower wasted traffic.
Then, these approaches account for the highly dynamic nature of the IoV and the volatility of vehicular communication channels, which traditional models could not effectively capture.

One representative direction is reinforcement learning applied to RSU-level caching.

Federated learning has also been explored to balance privacy with prediction accuracy.

This method used distributed echo-state networks enhanced with federated learning to predict both content demand and mobility patterns. DeepFESL significantly increased cache utilization and reduced delivery latency while preserving user privacy.

Another promising direction is hierarchical or hybrid federated learning for autonomous vehicles.

They argued that focusing only on popularity can bias cache contents toward homogeneous items, reducing user satisfaction. By combining latent Dirichlet allocation with contrastive learning, they optimized content placement for both hit rate and diversity.

Collectively, these works substantiate the efficacy of ML through reinforcement learning, LSTM prediction, or federated hybrid models in enhancing the performance of vehicular precaching.
However, there are still limitations to be addressed.
Firstly, many approaches rely on either centralized or semi-centralized learning, which introduces communication overhead and potential network congestion.
Secondly, supervised models often require continuous data streaming from vehicles, which consumes scarce wireless resources.
Thirdly, even advanced predictors can mispredict real-world dwell times, leading to wasted precached chunks.
These persistent challenges indicate that while ML has advanced vehicular caching, further research is required to formulate distributed, lightweight, and context-rich models that can optimize prediction accuracy while maintaining resource efficiency.



Based on the design and implementation described above, the main achievements can be summarized to clearly understand the aims of this paper.  
Below, we outline the five primary contributions of this work:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Snapshot-based Efficient Data Collection:}
    Unlike traditional time-series forecasting that demands continuous trajectory tracking, our approach utilizes instantaneous communication snapshots captured only during request events.
    This strategy significantly reduces wireless congestion and data overhead by eliminating the need for constant periodic reporting from vehicles.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty-aware Generative Modeling with ST-CVAE:}
    We propose a ST-CVAE that explicitly models the multi-modal distribution of dwell times.
    By quantifying predictive uncertainty through conformal prediction, the system sets conservative precaching thresholds ($n+\delta$) that minimize backhaul waste while ensuring service continuity.
    \item \textbf{Decentralized and Localized Learning:}
    We implement a fully decentralized learning framework where each RSU independently trains its model using local data.
    This allows the system to adapt to specific regional traffic patterns and signal schedules without the latency, privacy risks, or communication costs associated with centralized server aggregation.
    \item \textbf{Seamless Model Update Mechanism:}
    To guaranty service availability, we design an asynchronous model update protocol.
    RSUs perform heavy training tasks in the background while the existing model continues to serve requests.
    Once training is complete, the new model hot-swaps the old one, ensuring zero downtime during updates.
    \item \textbf{Lightweight Precaching Protocol Design:}
    We develop a practical communication protocol that requires minimal information exchange between adjacent RSUs.
    The protocol efficiently gathers next-hop context and coordinates chunk allocation, ensuring that the proposed scheme is deployable on resource-constrained edge infrastructure.
\end{itemize}


\section{Network Model and Scheme Overview} \label{sec.net}
In this section, we first present the considered network model and then describe the overview of the proposed scheme in the following subsections, respectively.

\subsection{Vehicular Network Environment}
In this subsection, we describe the considered network model, which consists of the vehicular environment, the communication infrastructure, and the content delivery and precaching mechanism.
The model integrates CCN-based message exchange with ML-driven dwell time prediction and includes mechanisms to monitor average speed and communication throughput at each RSU to support accurate and adaptive precaching.

Vehicles request data via nearby RSUs, which are interconnected by a high-speed fiber backhaul to content servers.
Vehicles move through a Manhattan-style grid of city streets, following shortest paths toward random destinations as defined by a SUMO traffic simulation.
Each RSU is a fixed wireless access point deployed along the roads (e.g., at intersections) that provides dedicated short-range communication coverage (802.11p WAVE) to vehicles within a few hundred meters.
As a vehicle enters an RSU's coverage, it associates with that RSU and can exchange messages while moving in the range.
All RSUs serve as relays between vehicles and the broader network, and they are interconnected through a reliable wired backhaul linking them to each other and to a central content server.
This infrastructure ensures that when a vehicle requests content, the request can be serviced either locally or via rapid fetch from other RSUs or the content server over fiber.
The high capacity and low latency of the wired backbone, combined with caching at the RSUs, alleviates strain on the core network by keeping popular content closer to the edge.

To reflect dynamic traffic conditions, each RSU maintains two local monitoring mechanisms.
First, the RSU tracks the average vehicle speed in its coverage.
For every vehicle, the entry and exit times are recorded in the RSU's queue.
When the queue reaches its maximum size, older records are discarded.
The RSU derives the average speed by dividing the average dwell time of vehicles by the communication diameter of its coverage.
Second, the RSU tracks the average communication speed.
Each time a vehicle downloads content, the measured throughput is stored in a queue up to a fixed maximum size.
The average value is then computed and used as an indicator of available link capacity.
This value shows the average number of requester vehicles within its coverage.
These two statistics, the average vehicle speed and the average communication speed, are continuously updated and supplied as input features to the prediction model.
They are also used directly when calculating the amount of content deliverable during a predicted dwell time and the quantity of content that should be proactively cached at the next RSU.

\subsection{CCN-based Content Delivery and Precaching Protocol}
The proposed system adopts a CCN paradigm for vehicle content requests.
In this model, all data is referenced by content names rather than host addresses, and each RSU employs the standard CCN packet handling structures: 
1) a Content Store (CS) for caching; 
2) a Pending Interest Table (PIT) for tracking outstanding requests; 
3) a Forwarding Information Base (FIB) for routing interests.

When a smart vehicle's application requires a content item (e.g., a video segment or map update), it issues an \(Interest\) packet naming that content, which is routed to the local RSU via WAVE.
Upon receiving an \(Interest\) packet, the RSU checks its CS for a cached copy of the content.
If the content is found locally, the RSU immediately returns a \(Data\) packet to the vehicle, satisfying the request with minimal latency.
If the content is not in the local cache, the RSU creates a PIT entry to remember the requesting vehicle and interface and forwards the \(Interest\) packet over the fiber backhaul using its FIB.
The FIB directs the \(Interest\) packet either to a neighboring RSU that is likely to have the content or to the central content server, following the content-centric routing policies.
Because all RSUs are interconnected and share the content-centric naming scheme, any RSU with the data can respond.
The first node (another RSU or the server) that has the content will return a \(Data\) packet following the reverse path (PIT state ensures it traces back to the requesting RSU and vehicle).
As the \(Data\) packet travels back, each intermediate RSU may cache a copy in its CS for future use and inform its cached status to neighboring RSUs to update their FIBs.
Finally, the local RSU delivers the \(Data\) packet to the vehicle, satisfying the origin request.
This CCN approach naturally supports in-network caching and reduces redundant data transmissions, since popular content may be served by the nearest cache rather than always from the origin.

\paragraph{Local Monitoring of Vehicular and Communication States}
Each RSU continuously maintains two statistical indicators to enhance its prediction capability for dwell time and content transmission.
First, the RSU records the entry and exit times of all vehicles within its coverage to derive the average vehicular speed $v_{av}$ over the last hour.
This moving average is periodically updated every minute to reflect the most recent traffic conditions.
Second, the RSU tracks its average communication throughput $C_{av}$ over the past hour by logging the instantaneous throughput allocated to each requester.
Both metrics are maintained using a sliding-window approach and discarded beyond a one-hour horizon to avoid outdated information.

\section{Proposed Scheme} \label{sec.prop}
This section presents the proposed RSU-driven precaching scheme designed to support seamless content delivery in CIoV environments.
The proposed scheme leverages locally learned dwell-time estimation models to enable proactive yet conservative precaching decisions without relying on any centralized controller.
Each RSU independently performs feature acquisition, inference, precaching execution, and model updating based solely on locally available information and limited coordination with neighboring RSUs.
This decentralized design reduces backhaul dependency, preserves data locality, and improves scalability under dense vehicular traffic.
The overall workflow of the proposed scheme is organized into four tightly coupled stages.
First, subsection~\ref{subsec:1} describes how an RSU handles incoming content requests and constructs contextual feature vectors by collecting both local observations and neighboring RSU information.
These features are stored as incomplete samples in a local Dataset Table and serve as inputs to the prediction model.
Subsection~\ref{subsec:2} introduces the ST-CVAE framework, which explicitly models the stochastic and multi-modal nature of vehicular dwell time by learning the conditional probability distribution from the acquired features.
Based on the estimated dwell times, subsection~\ref{subsec:3} explains how each RSU determines whether precaching is necessary and executes the corresponding precaching operation to support seamless RSU handover.
Finally, subsection~\ref{subsec:4} details how actual mobility outcomes are reported by vehicles to complete labeled training samples and how each RSU updates its local prediction model asynchronously without interrupting ongoing content delivery.


\subsection{Request Handling and Feature Acquisition}\label{subsec:1}
When a vehicle generates an \(Interest\) packet for content, it includes not only the standard content identifier but also the vehicle's current mobility state and a \(chunk\_no\) field.
The \(chunk\_no\) serves to distinguish between new sessions (0) and ongoing streams ($>0$), enabling accurate feedback tracking later.
Upon receiving the request, the RSU $R_{cur}$ first attempts to serve content from its local cache following standard CCN procedures described in Section \ref{sec.net}. 
However, simultaneously with this delivery process, $R_{cur}$ triggers the predictive precaching module.
The primary task of this module is to construct a comprehensive feature vector that captures the mobility context of the requester vehicle, including its current position $P_{req}$, velocity $v_{req}$, and travel direction $dir_{req}$.
Specifically, $dir_{req}$ is defined as a binary indicator, set to 1 if the vehicle has not yet crossed the intersection, and 0 if it has already passed through it.

To build a complete feature vector, $R_{cur}$ first collects its local environmental data, such as the number of concurrently connected vehicles $N_{cur}$, the average vehicular speed $v_{av, cur}$, the average communication throughput $C_{av, cur}$, and the current traffic signal state $S_{cur}$ along with the remaining time until the next phase $t_{sig, cur}$.
Subsequently, it appends the requester vehicle's spatial parameters: the remaining distance to exit $R_{cur}$ ($D_{lev, cur}$), the distance to enter $R_{nxt}$ ($D_{arr, nxt}$), and the distance to exit $R_{nxt}$ ($D_{lev, nxt}$).
However, these local data alone are insufficient for accurate dwell time prediction.
Hence, after storing the local data in its data table alongside the identifier of the requester vehicle, $R_{cur}$ initiates a distributed information exchange with the next RSU $R_{nxt}$ to gather next-hop contextual data.
Through a backhaul connection, $R_{cur}$ sends an \(Info Request\) packet containing the identifiers of the requester vehicle and $R_{cur}$.
Upon receiving this packet, $R_{nxt}$ contacts all RSUs connected by incoming road segments leading into its coverage area.
Each neighboring RSU examines its own communication range and counts the number of vehicles that are currently expected to move toward $R_{nxt}$.

%\begin{figure}[htp] \centering
%    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./figure/feature.png}
%    \caption{The completed feature vector.} \label{fig:feature}
%\end{figure}
Then, it returns this information through an \(Info Reply\) packet.
After receiving all replies, $R_{nxt}$ compiles the data, including the number of incoming vehicles from each direction $\sum N_{n\rightarrow nxt}$, the total number of vehicles within its own coverage $N_{nxt}$, the average vehicular speed $v_{av, nxt}$, the average communication throughput $C_{av, nxt}$, and the current traffic signal state $S_{nxt}$ along with the remaining time until the next phase $t_{sig, nxt}$.
This aggregated data is encapsulated into a single \(Info\) packet including the stored identifier of the requester vehicle and $R_{cur}$, which is transmitted back to $R_{cur}$ through the wired backhaul.
Once $R_{cur}$ receives the $Info$ packet, it merges the next-hop data with the stored local data in its data table based on the identifier of the requester vehicle to complete the feature vector.
At this stage, the feature set represents the spatial and temporal dynamics of both the current and the next RSU, including mobility, connectivity, and traffic signal parameters.
% Formally, the completed feature vector can be expressed as shown in Figure \ref{fig:feature}.
The completed feature vector is stored in the Dataset Table, indexed by the vehicle ID and content ID.
If the request represents the start of a new session (\(chunk\_no = 0\)), the RSU proceeds with content delivery and prediction but does not expect a \(Result\) packet later.
For ongoing sessions, this entry will later be paired with the actual time reported from the next RSU when the vehicle departs, completing a labeled dataset for supervised model updates.


\subsection{Uncertainty-aware generative modeling framework}\label{subsec:2}
In CIoV, optimizing the volume of precached data chunks at an RSU fundamentally depends on the accurate prediction of the vehicle's dwell time, denoted as the target variable $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{+}$.
This prediction relies on the comprehensive feature vector $X$ constructed in Subsection \ref{subsec:1}. The vector integrates numerical features (e.g., vehicular velocity, distance to RSU exit, communication throughput), categorical features (e.g., RSU ID, travel direction), and cyclic temporal features (e.g., remaining traffic light phase time).
Existing predictive models typically formulate this task as a deterministic mapping $\hat{Y}=f(X)$ aimed at minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE).
However, mathematically the MSE forces the model to predict the conditional expected value $\mathbb{E}[Y|X]$.

\begin{figure}[htp] \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./graph/Uncertainty.png}
    \caption{Empirical conditional probability density of dwell times according to the distance to the next RSU coverage exit. Distributions are presented for (a) current dwell time and (b) next dwell time, partitioned into ten equal-frequency deciles based on the remaining distance from the vehicle's current location to the next RSU coverage boundary. Each decile contains an equal number of observations, with the corresponding physical distance intervals indicated in parentheses.} \label{fig:uncertainty}
\end{figure}
As empirically demonstrated in Fig. \ref{fig:uncertainty}, urban traffic dynamics are inherently stochastic, and the actual dwell time distribution conditioned on the distance to an intersection exhibits severe conditional multi-modality.
For instance, vehicles at the exact same distance might either pass through a green light (forming a short dwell time mode) or stop at a red light (forming a long dwell time mode).
By predicting the mathematical average of these modes, deterministic models output a medium dwell time that falls into a low-probability region between the peaks.
In CIoV precaching, this mode averaging error is catastrophic: underestimating the long mode leads to service stalls, while overestimating the short mode causes massive backhaul traffic waste.
To address this, we redefine the problem as conditional density estimation $p(Y|X)$ and propose a ST-CVAE to explicitly capture the true multi-modal distribution without averaging the underlying dynamics.

% \subsubsection{ST-CVAE Architecture}
The overall architecture of the proposed ST-CVAE is illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:arch}.
Rather than relying on generic off-the-shelf neural networks, the ST-CVAE incorporates custom modules meticulously designed to process heterogeneous vehicular tabular data and extract meaningful spatio-temporal representations.
To accurately predict vehicle dwell times, the model must seamlessly comprehend periodic temporal features, primarily the remaining phase time of traffic lights.
In conventional approaches, this scalar time $t$ is directly fed into the network.
However, utilizing a raw linear timer over a cycle length $T$ creates a mathematical discontinuity at the phase boundaries--namely, the abrupt numerical collapse from $T$ back to zero.
This topological disruption severely destabilizes gradient descent optimization.
To resolve this structural flaw, we propose a CYE module.
This module mathematically maps the one-dimensional scalar time into a two-dimensional trigonometric space using the transformation $(\sin(2\pi t/T), \cos(2\pi t/T))$.
By projecting the temporal feature onto a continuous unit circle, this encoding rigorously preserves topological continuity.
Consequently, the neural network can smoothly learn the periodic nature of traffic signal transitions without being misdirected by artificial boundary drops.

Following the temporal encoding, the architecture requires a robust mechanism to capture complex inter-dependencies among heterogeneous tabular inputs, which encompass spatial coordinates, numerical velocities, and categorical travel direction.
Unlike image or text data, these tabular features inherently lack spatial locality and translation invariance.
Standard Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) process these inputs through simple additive combinations followed by conventional activation functions (e.g., ReLU).
This purely additive mechanism fundamentally restricts the network's capacity to learn explicit feature interactions.
To overcome this limitation, we designed a custom Deep Residual Backbone (ResBlock).
Each ResBlock sequences layer normalization, a linear projection, a Rectified Gated Linear Unit (ReGLU) activation \cite{x1}, dropout, and a final linear projection, all connected via a residual path.
The mathematical superiority of this structural design for vehicular tabular data is formalized as follows.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (Representation efficiency of ReGLU-augmented ResBlock).}
Let $x$ be the intermediate tabular representation within the network.
While our ResBlock splits the representation $x$ into two halves and computes $x_{1}\bigodot ReLU(x_{2})$, where $\bigodot$ denotes the Hadamard product.
This mathematical operation explicitly models non-linear multiplicative interactions, allowing the network to naturally learn synergistic spatio-temporal effects (e.g., the multiplied combinatorial impact of vehicle velocity and cyclical signal phase).
Furthermore, the residual connection $x'=x+\mathcal{F}(x)$ ensures that the Jacobian matrix of the layer incorporates an identity matrix $I$.
This strictly bounds the singular values of the gradients near one, effectively mitigating the vanishing gradient problem inherent in deep tabular networks and guaranteeing stable optimization dynamics.

\textbf{Probabilistic Generative Formulation.}
Even with optimal feature extraction via the ResBlock backbone, unobservable factors (e.g., hidden traffic congestion upstream, sudden driver hesitation) inject irreducible stochasticity into the dwell time.
To encapsulate this unobservable noise, we introduce a stochastic latent variable $Z\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}$.

\textbf{Definition 1 (Conditional latent variable model).}
The generative process assumes that the target dwell time $Y$ is produced from the joint distribution $p_{\theta, \Psi}(Y,Z|X)=p_{\theta}(Y|X|Z)p_{\Psi}(Z|X)$.
Here, $p_{\Psi}(Z|X)$ is the prior encoder parameterized by $\Psi$ that infers the latent distribution solely from $X$, and $p_{\theta}(Y|X,Z)$ is the generative decoder parameterized by $\theta$.
However, directly optimizing this likelihood requires calculating the true posterior distribution $p(Z|X,Y)=\cfrac{p_{\theta}(Y|X,Z)p_{\Psi}(Z|X)}{p(Y|X)}$, which us analytically intractable.
Thus, we approximate it using a parameterized posterior encoder $q_{\Phi}(Z^{*}|X,Y)$ that observes both $X$ and $Y$ during the training phase.

\textbf{Theorem 1 ($\beta$-ELBO objective).}
The marginal log-likelihood of the observed data $\log p(Y|X)$ is strictly lower-bounded by the modified Evidence Lower Bound ($\beta$-ELBO), denoted as $\mathcal{L}_{\beta}$.
Maximizing this objective minimizes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the approximate and true posteriors, compelling the decoder to generate multi-modal distributions.
\begin{equation}\begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{\beta}=&\mathbb{E}_{Z^{*}\sim q_{\Psi}(Z^{*}|X,Y)} \left[ \log p_{\theta}(Y|X,Z^{*})  \right]\\
    & - \beta D_{KL} \left[ q_{\Phi}(Z^{*}|X,Y)||p_{\Psi}(Z|X)\right].
\end{split}\end{equation}

\textbf{Proof.}
By decomposing the marginal log-likelihood using the exact mathematical definition of the KL divergence between the variational approximation $q_{\Phi}$ and the true posterior $p(Z|X,Y)$, we obtain:
\begin{equation} \begin{split}
    D_{KL}\left(q_{\Phi}||p(Z|X,Y)\right)&=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Phi}}\left[\log q_{\Phi}(Z^{*}|X,Y) \cfrac{}{}\right.\\
    & \left. - \log\left( \cfrac{p_{\theta}(Y|X,Z^{{*}})p_{\Psi}(Z|X)}{p(Y|X)} \right) \right].
\end{split}\end{equation}
Rearranging the terms yields:
\begin{equation}\begin{split}
    &\log p(Y|X) - D_{KL}\left(q_{\Phi}||p(Z|X,Y)\right) \\
    & = \mathbb{E}_{q_{\Phi}}\left[ \log p_{\theta}(Y|X,Z^{*}) \right] - D_{KL}[q_{\Phi}(Z^{*}|X,Y)||p_{\Psi}(Z|X)].
\end{split}\end{equation}
Since the KL divergence is strictly non-negative ($D_{KL}\ge 0$), the right side of the equation forms a rigorous lower bound on $\log p(Y|X)$.
The hyperparameter $\beta$ is introduced as a weighting factor to regulate the capacity of the latent bottleneck, preventing posterior collapse--a critical issue when training generative models on tabular data.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (Gaussian reparameterization).}
To make the stochastic sampling process differentiable, both encoders are designed to output the parameters of a multivariate Gaussian: the mean vector $\mu$ and standard deviation vector $\sigma$.
We employ the reparameterization trick $Z^{*}=\mu_{\Phi}+\sigma_{\Phi}\bigodot\epsilon$, where $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ is an independent standard Gaussian noise vector.
This isolates the non-differentiable stochasticity into $\epsilon$, allowing deterministic gradients to backpropagate through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ for end-to-end optimization.


\subsection{Precaching Decision and Execution} \label{subsec:3}
This subsection explains how the precaching decisions determined in the previous stage are executed in practice to ensure seamless content delivery during RSU handover.
Given the dwell time prediction results produced by the trained local prediction model, $R_{cur}$ determines whether precaching is required for the ongoing content session.
If the predicted remaining dwell time at the $R_{cur}$, denoted as $t_{dw,cur}$, is sufficient to transmit the entire requested content before the vehicle departs, the content delivery proceeds following the standard CCN mechanism without invoking precaching.
In this case, all content chunks are delivered directly from $R_{cur}$, and no coordination with $R_{nxt}$ is necessary.
%
When the predicted dwell time at $R_{cur}$ is insufficient to complete content delivery, 
$R_{cur}$ computes how many chunks can be delivered locally and how many must be prepared in advance at the next RSU $R_{nxt}$.
This decision relies on the dwell time estimates and the locally maintained average communication throughput.
Specifically, the number of chunks that can be transmitted within the current RSU coverage is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    m = \cfrac{C_{av, cur} \times t_{dw, cur}}{S_{chunk}}, \label{eq:m}
\end{equation}
where $C_{av, cur}$ denotes the average communication throughput at $R_{cur}$ and $S_{chunk}$ is the size of each content chunk.
Based on the predicted arrival and departure times at $R_{nxt}$, the number of chunks expected to be delivered within the coverage of $R_{nxt}$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    n = \cfrac{C_{av, nxt} \times \left( t_{lev, nxt} - t_{arr, nxt} \right)}{S_{chunk}}, \label{eq:n}
\end{equation}
where $C_{av, nxt}$ represents the average communication throughput at $R_{nxt}$.
Collectively, $m$ and $n$ determine the allocation boundary between chunks delivered at $R_{cur}$.
%
To account for residual estimation uncertainty, a safety buffer denoted as the \textit{micro-base} ($\delta$) is explicitly added to the calculated requirement.
While $n$ represents the expected number of chunks deliverable during the predicted dwell time at $R_{nxt}$, the actual precaching instruction requests $N_{precache}$ chunks, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    N_{precache} = n + \delta, \label{eq:n_precache}
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is a bounded integer (e.g., 5-10 chunks) sufficient to cover minor arrival time deviations.
Consequently, the \(Precaching Instruction\) packet generated by $R_{cur}$ requests the range of chunks corresponding to $m$ (served locally) up to $m + n + \delta$ (preloaded at $R_{nxt}$). This explicit buffering ensures that even if handover occurs earlier than predicted, the vehicle encounters no service gap.

After determining the chunk allocation, $R_{cur}$ generates a \(Precaching Instruction\) packet and transmits it to $R_{nxt}$ via the fiber backhaul.
The packet contains the vehicle ID, content ID, the starting chunk number, and the total number of chunks to be precached.
Upon receiving the instruction, $R_{nxt}$ checks its Content Store to verify whether the specified chunks are already cached.
If the chunks are unavailable, $R_{nxt}$ issues corresponding \(Interest\) packet following the standard CCN forwarding procedure.
These requests may be satisfied by neighboring RSUs or, if necessary, by the content server.
All retrieved chunks are cached at $R_{nxt}$ before the vehicle enters its coverage area.
%
Meanwhile, $R_{cur}$ continues delivering the first $m$ chunks to the vehicle without interruption.
When the vehicle exits $R_{cur}$ and enters the coverage of $R_{nxt}$, content delivery seamlessly resumes from the precached chunks.
Since the required chunks, including the micro-base, are already stored at $R_{nxt}$, the vehicle can immediately receive \(Data\) packets in response to its \(Interest\) packet.
This implicit handover eliminates access delay and avoids explicit session migration.
%
If the vehicle remains at $R_{nxt}$ longer than conservatively estimated, additional chunks beyond the precached range can be retrieved through standard CCN forwarding.
This situation corresponds to normal cache-miss handling and does not compromise service continuity, as the initial precached content guarantees uninterrupted delivery during the handover phase.
Through this execution process, the proposed scheme translates dwell time estimation results  into effective precaching actions while balancing service continuity and traffic efficiency.



\subsection{Feedback Reporting and Local Model Update}\label{subsec:4}
This subsection describes how actual dwell time measurements are collected and reported to construct labeled training samples, and how each RSU updates its local prediction model without disrupting ongoing content delivery.
Unlike centralized learning schemes that require continuous mobility tracking or raw data aggregation, the proposed framework adopts an event-driven feedback mechanism in which the requesting vehicle itself acts as a lightweight label generator.
This design significantly reduces signaling overhead while preserving the accuracy and reliability of ground-truth annotations.
%
During content delivery within the coverage of $R_{cur}$, the vehicle locally records the start time associated with each content request.
Each request is uniquely identified by the tuple $(VID, CID, chunk\_no)$, which corresponds to an independent inference instance stored at the RSU.
Accordingly, the vehicle maintains a separate timer for each active request.
This design guarantees that dwell time measurements remain unambiguous even when multiple content requests for the same vehicle and content coexist with different starting chunk numbers.
%
When the vehicle exits the coverage of $R_{cur}$, it records the actual departure time corresponding to each active request.
Subsequently, upon entering the coverage of the next RSU $R_{nxt}$, the vehicle records the arrival time at $R_{nxt}$.
Finally, when the vehicle leaves the coverage of $R_{nxt}$, it records the departure time from $R_{nxt}$.
Collectively, these timestamps constitute the ground-truth mobility information required to evaluate the accuracy of the dwell-time estimation used during precaching decisions.

After leaving the coverage of $R_{nxt}$, the vehicle generates a \(Result\) message that encapsulates the measured timing information.
The report includes the vehicle ID, content ID, the identifier of the previous RSU $R_{cur}$, the requested chunk number, the actual dwell time at $R_{cur}$, the arrival time at $R_{nxt}$, and the departure time from $R_{nxt}$.
At the time of report generation, the RSU currently serving the vehicle acts as the new current RSU.
Therefore, the report is first delivered to this RSU and subsequently forwarded to the previous RSU $R_{cur}$ through the backhaul network.
%
Upon receiving the \(Result\) packet, $R_{cur}$ uses the tuple $(VID, CID, chunk\_no)$ to locate the corresponding incomplete entry in its Dataset Table.
Since each inference instance is stored independently based on the requested chunk number, the RSU can uniquely match the reported actual values with the previously stored feature vector and predicted dwell-time outputs.
The dataset entry is then completed by appending the actual dwell time measurements, forming a labeled training sample consisting of input features, model predictions, and observed outcomes.
%
Each RSU accumulates completed training samples in a local buffer.
When the number of stored samples reaches a predefined batch threshold, the RSU initiates a local model update procedure.
To ensure that content delivery and precaching operations remain uninterrupted, model training is executed asynchronously in a separate background thread.
During this process, the RSU continues to handle incoming content requests and perform dwell time prediction using the currently deployed model.

Once the training process is completed, the updated model seamlessly replaces the previous one.
All subsequent requests are processed using the updated model parameters, while requests that arrived during the training phase remain unaffected.
This asynchronous update mechanism ensures service continuity and avoids introducing additional latency or instability during learning.
%
Through this feedback-driven learning loop, each RSU continuously refines its local prediction model using directly observed mobility behavior.
As a result, the learned models naturally adapt to location-specific traffic patterns, intersection characteristics, and communication conditions.
By decoupling learning from content delivery and avoiding centralized data aggregation, the proposed scheme achieves scalable, privacy-preserving, and robust online learning tailored to highly dynamic vehicular environments.

\textbf{Implementation details.}
All experimental pipelines were implemented using the PyTorch framework and executed on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.
The ML baselines utilized scikit-learn library along with their respective official open-source libraries \cite{a1, a2, a3}.
The DL baselines leveraged their official implementations provided by the original authors \cite{a4, a5, a6}.

All DL baselines, including ST-CVAE, were optimized using the AdamW optimizer \cite{x9} for a maximum of 200 epochs with a batch size of 512, employing early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs.
To ensure a strictly fair evaluation of uncertainty quantification (UQ), deterministic DL models (MLP, ResNet, FTT, TabR) were fundamentally modified.
Their single-node output layers were expanded to simultaneously predict three distinct quantiles (lower, median, and upper), and the conventional MSE loss was replaced by a Pinball loss \cite{x10}.
This architectural adaptation enables the application of conformalized quantile regression (CQR) \cite{x11}.
Conversely, NGBoost, TabPFN, and the proposed ST-CVAE inherently output full probability distributions; thus, CQR was seamlessly applied to their generated predictive distributions without requiring architectural modifications.
Finally, to eliminate hyperparameter bias, the Optuna framework \cite{x12} was utilized to execute 30 trials for every ML and DL baseline, guaranteeing all comparative evaluations were conducted under their optimal configurations.

Table \ref{tab:table2} details the quantitative evaluation results, including prediction accuracy ($R^{2}$-score), Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) \cite{x13}, and Normalized Mean Prediction Interval Width (NMPIW) \cite{x14}.
Here, PICP and NMPIW are representative measures to evaluate the reliability of uncertainty intervals.
As theorized in Section \ref{subsec:2}, deterministic ML and DL models inherently suffer from mode averaging on multi-modal traffic data, mathematically stalling their $R^{2}$-scores.
The ST-CVAE mitigates this fundamental limitation by learning the unobserved traffic dynamics via the generative latent variables Z, achieving the highest $R^{2}$-score (0.8153 for dwell time) and decisively outperforming state-of-the-art models.
Furthermore, assessing the UQ calibration, the ST-CVAE perfectly satisfies the nominal 90\% confidence target with a PICP of 0.9001, while simultaneously maintaining an extremely tight NMPIW of 0.0728.
In CIoV precaching operations, this indicates that our model provides mathematically guaranteed safety bounds without excessively widening the predictive intervals, directly enabling the RSU to minimize wasted backhaul bandwidth.

\textbf{Out-of-distribution analysis.}
The safety of a CIoV system relies on the model’s awareness of its own ignorance. This epistemic self-awareness is validated in Figure \ref{fig:graph4}.
To rigorously evaluate the model under unrecorded anomalies, we partitioned the testing dataset, defining out-of-distribution (OOD) samples as the extreme top and bottom 10\% of the dwell time and next dwell time distributions, designating the remaining 80\% as in-distribution (ID).
As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:graph4}, when evaluating unfamiliar OOD samples, the sampling variance ($\sum \sigma^{2}_{\Psi}$) of the ST-CVAE’s prior encoder spikes compared to the stable ID samples. This clear mathematical separation demonstrates that the ST-CVAE explicitly recognizes anomalous traffic conditions–-it “knows what it does not know.” In an operational CIoV architecture, when the RSU detects this variance spike, it can instantly abort risky precaching decisions and trigger a proactive fail-safe fallback to macro-cellular networks, thereby guaranteeing uninterrupted service continuity.

\subsection{Network Performance Evaluation} \label{sec.s2}
This section evaluates the system-level impact of the proposed precaching scheme.
We compare our ST-CVAE based approach against the ten baseline models verified in Subsection \ref{sec.s1} (i.e., LR, RF, XGBoost, CatBoost, NGBoost, MLP, ResNet, FTT, TabR, and TabPFN).
In all scenarios, the underlying CCN communication protocol and network topology remain identical; only the dwell time prediction model and the corresponding precaching decision logic vary.
Specifically, deterministic models (LR, RF, XGBoost, CatBoost, MLP, ResNet, FTT, TabR) utilize point estimates for precaching, while probabilistic models (NGBoost, TabPFN) and our ST-CVAE leverage the predicted distribution to determine the optimal precaching volume.

To assess the effectiveness of the schemes, we continuously measured two key performance metrics during the simulation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Access Delay:} The average time elapsed between a vehicle issuing an \(Interest\) packet and receiving the first chunk of the content.
    This metric directly reflects service latency and user experience.
    \item \textbf{Wasted Traffic:} The total volume of content chunks that were precached at RSUs but were never consumed by the target vehicles (e.g., due to early departure or path deviation).
    This metric indicates the efficiency of backhaul resource usage.
\end{itemize}

Both metrics are heavily influenced by the accuracy of dwell time prediction.
Underestimated dwell times lead to insufficient precaching and higher access delays at the next RSU, while overestimated dwell times result in excessive precaching and wasted backhaul bandwidth.

\begin{figure} \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./graph/NetPerf.png}
    \caption{Network performance evaluation comparison: (a) Overall average access delay across models, (b) Overall wasted traffic volume, (c) Access delay variation with respect to vehicle speed, and (d) Wasted traffic volume variation with respect to vehicle speed.} \label{fig:g1}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:g1} presents the comprehensive network performance results, comparing the proposed ST-CVAE scheme against ten baseline models including CatBoost, FTT, LR, MLP, NGBoost, ResNet, RF, TabPFN, TabR, and XGBoost.
Figures \ref{fig:g1}(a) and (b) illustrate the overall average access delay and wasted traffic volume, respectively.
The proposed method consistently achieves the lowest access delay among all schemes, demonstrating its superior capability in accurately predicting dwell times and ensuring seamless content delivery during handovers.
Although the introduction of a safety buffer (micro-base $\delta$) induces a slight increase in potential traffic overhead, Figure \ref{fig:g1}(b) confirms that the proposed scheme maintains a highly competitive level of wasted traffic, lower than deterministic models that suffer from frequent over-prediction.

Figures \ref{fig:g1}(c) and (d) further analyze the performance dynamics with respect to vehicle speed, which serves as a proxy for mobility uncertainty.
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:g1}(c), access delay generally decreases as vehicle speed increases but exhibits a parabolic trend.
At low speeds (below 30 km/h), vehicles are heavily influenced by traffic signals and congestion, leading to high dwell time volatility and increased prediction difficulty, which in turn elevates access delay for all models.
Conversely, at high speeds (above 60 km/h), the latency between the snapshot capture and actual arrival becomes significant, introducing prediction errors due to rapid state changes.
Notably, while linear models like LR show a continuous increase in delay with speed due to their inability to capture non-linear dynamics, the proposed ST-CVAE maintains the lowest delay across the entire speed range, highlighting its robustness.

Regarding wasted traffic in Figure \ref{fig:g1}(d), we observe a general downward trend as vehicle speed increases.
Faster vehicles have shorter dwell times, naturally reducing the total volume of chunks that can vary between valid precaching and waste.
However, a distinct crossover point is observed at approximately 32.5 km/h.
Below this threshold, the standard MLP appears to waste less traffic than the proposed scheme.
However, this is not due to superior efficiency but rather under-prediction, as evidenced by MLP's higher access delay in the same speed range (Fig. \ref{fig:g1}(c)), indicating that it failed to precache enough content.
Beyond 32.5 km/h, the proposed ST-CVAE achieves lower wasted traffic than MLP while maintaining superior service quality, proving that its uncertainty-aware decision logic balances the trade-off between latency and resource efficiency.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec.c}
In this paper, we proposed a novel V2I precaching scheme based on a Spatio-Temporal Conditional Variational Autoencoder to address the challenges of content delivery in highly dynamic urban vehicular networks.
Unlike traditional deterministic approaches, our scheme models the multi-modal distribution of vehicle dwell times, capturing distinct traffic behaviors such as stopping at red lights or passing through intersections.
By leveraging a snapshot-based data collection strategy, we minimized wireless communication overhead while maintaining high prediction accuracy through cyclical temporal encoding and a specialized residual backbone.
Furthermore, we introduced an uncertainty-aware precaching decision algorithm that utilizes conformal prediction intervals to determine conservative yet sufficient data chunks for preloading.
Simulation results demonstrated that the proposed scheme outperforms existing baseline models, achieving the lowest average access delay and reducing wasted backhaul traffic across various vehicle speeds.
% In particular, the decentralized learning and asynchronous update mechanism ensured robust adaptability to local traffic conditions without service interruption.
